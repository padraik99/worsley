Sight log

    How to use the pocket sextant
    • Zero it first. Aim the crosshair at a sea horizon (or any distant flat horizon) and tap Zero. That's your index correction, same idea as on a real sextant.
    • Take a sight. Hold the crosshair on the Sun (camera) or sight along the phone's edge at a star, and tap Take sight. Steady your elbows; the reading is averaged over about a second.
    • Noon sight. Start a few minutes before local noon, take a sight every minute or so, and keep going until the altitude starts falling. The highest one is your noon sight: its altitude gives latitude, its time gives longitude.
    • Polaris. In the northern hemisphere the altitude of Polaris is your latitude to within about ¾°.
    • Check against GPS only after you've committed to an answer. Worsley didn't get a reveal button.

    The sextant

    A sextant measures one thing: the angle between two objects you can see. Usually those are the Sun (or a star) and the horizon. Everything else in celestial navigation is arithmetic on that single number plus the time.

    What it is

    A wedge-shaped frame, one sixth of a circle (hence sextant), carrying two mirrors and a small telescope. A movable arm swings across a scale engraved on the arc. You look at the horizon through the telescope, swing the arm until the Sun appears to sit on that horizon, and read the angle off the scale.

    The trick that makes it work at sea

    Try to measure the Sun's height with a protractor and plumb line on a rolling deck and the plumb line swings uselessly. The sextant sidesteps the problem with double reflection. The Sun's light hits the index mirror, bounces to the horizon mirror, and bounces again into your eye, where it is overlaid on the direct view of the horizon.

    Because both images arrive through the same eyepiece, rocking the instrument moves them together. It's like lining up two photos on a single sheet of tracing paper: shake the sheet all you like, the alignment holds. That is why a navigator can get a reading from a deck that is pitching ten degrees.

    Double reflection also doubles the angle: turn the mirror 1° and the reflected ray turns 2°. So a 60° arc is engraved to read 120°.

    From angle to position

    At any instant the Sun is directly overhead exactly one spot on Earth. If you measure the Sun at 90°, you're standing on that spot. Measure 89° and you're somewhere on a circle 60 nautical miles from it. Every degree lower puts you 60 miles further out. That is not a coincidence: the nautical mile was defined as one arcminute of the Earth's surface, so one minute of sextant angle is one mile of ocean.

    Think of the Sun as a streetlamp and your angle as the length of your shadow: the shadow tells you how far you are from the lamp, though not in which direction. An almanac tells you where the lamp is. Two such circles from two sights cross at your position.

    The noon sight: latitude with almost no maths

    At local noon the Sun stops climbing, hangs for a moment, and starts down. Catch that peak altitude and:

    Latitude = 90° − altitude + Sun's declination

    Declination is the Sun's own latitude for that date, looked up in the almanac (this app computes it). No accurate clock needed, which is why the noon sight was the backbone of navigation for centuries.

    Longitude is a clock problem

    The Earth turns 15° an hour. If your chronometer, still keeping Greenwich time, says the Sun peaked at 14:00, you are 2 hours × 15° = 30° west of Greenwich (after a small seasonal correction called the equation of time). The catch: 4 seconds of clock error is 1 arcminute of longitude. A chronometer one minute wrong puts you up to 15 miles out.

    The small print

    A raw sextant reading gets corrected before use:

    • Index error — the instrument's own zero offset.
    • Dip — your eye is above the sea, so the visible horizon is slightly below true horizontal.
    • Refraction — air bends light upward; near the horizon bodies appear about half a degree higher than they are.
    • Semi-diameter — you rest the Sun's lower edge on the horizon, then add its radius (about 16′) to get the centre.

    A good sextant in practiced hands reads to a fraction of an arcminute, a fraction of a mile.

    Worsley and the James Caird

    Frank Worsley, captain of Endurance, navigated a 22½-foot lifeboat roughly 800 nautical miles from Elephant Island to South Georgia in April–May 1916, across the Southern Ocean in autumn. Consider what the method above demands, and what he had:

    • A visible Sun. By his own account he managed only four sights in sixteen days. The rest of the time the sky was overcast.
    • A visible horizon. From a small boat in a heavy swell the horizon is the top of the next wave. He took sights from the crests, braced upright by his shipmates, sometimes guessing at the Sun's edge through cloud.
    • An accurate clock. He had one working chronometer left, and no way to check it since leaving the ice.
    • Dry tables. His almanac and logarithm tables were pulp; pages had to be peeled apart.
    • Dead reckoning between sights. Course and speed estimated by eye in a boat thrown around by cross seas. Worsley called it "a merry jest of guesswork".

    South Georgia is about a hundred miles long. Miss it and the next land downwind was Africa, with no hope of beating back against the westerlies. He hit it. With four sights.

    How this app compares

    The Sextant tab uses your phone's accelerometer as the horizon reference instead of the visible horizon, the same idea as the "bubble sextants" used in aircraft. You aim; gravity supplies the horizontal. Expect errors of roughly half a degree to a degree, so a noon fix lands you within perhaps 30–60 miles. A real sextant is around a hundred times better. Worsley would not have traded.

    Compass notes

    • True vs magnetic. A compass points to the magnetic pole, not the geographic one. The difference (variation, or declination) depends on where you are. With a GPS fix the app applies the World Magnetic Model automatically; the model built in is valid through 2029.
    • Calibrate by waving the phone in a figure-eight for a few seconds if the heading seems off.
    • Keep clear of metal. Magnetic phone cases, car dashboards, steel desks and speakers will all pull the needle.
    • Sun check. The small gold disc on the dial shows where the Sun should bear. If the real Sun isn't over it, your compass is being disturbed. Navigators have checked compasses this way for centuries.
